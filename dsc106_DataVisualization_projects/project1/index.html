Why this matters

Processing and affordability are both constraints.

Healthy grocery decisions are not just about willpower. Shoppers compare shelf options under time, price, brand, package-size, and nutrition constraints. This project asks a narrower but useful question: when we compare listed products, how do processing and price trade off across three large retailers?

Unit of analysis: each row is a product listing, not a purchase. A cereal box and a snack pouch each count once, even if shoppers buy them at different rates.

1. The headline

Whole Foods has fewer ultra-processed listings, but calories cost more.

The original static graphic found a clear store-level tradeoff: Whole Foods lists a smaller ultra-processed share, while its median cost per 100 calories is much higher.

2. Processing mix

The biggest separation is in NOVA class 4.

Target and Walmart lean heavily toward ultra-processed products. Whole Foods still has many ultra-processed listings, but its shelf mix is less concentrated there.

3. Price

The price story is skewed, so medians matter.

Price per calorie has extreme outliers. The story uses medians and separately flags outliers so a few unusual products do not dominate the conclusion.

4. Nutrition

Processing is related to nutrition, but it is not the whole story.

FPro, sugar, sodium, fiber, protein, and calories are combined into a transparent exploratory Health Index below. It is meant for comparison, not medical advice.

Trust and methodology

How trustworthy is this insight?

The strongest signal is the store-level processing and median price comparison. The weakest parts are product comparability, missing nutrition, and the fact that assortment is not the same as consumer behavior.

    Key findings

    What I learned

    The main insight

    Whole Foods offers a less ultra-processed assortment, but the median calorie is more expensive.

    What cleaning changed

    Removing missing, zero, non-finite, and statistical price outliers makes the price comparison less volatile.

    What to be cautious about

    Products are weighted equally. The data does not measure sales volume, location, promotions, or shopper access.

    What this demonstrates

    D3 modules, coordinated views, brushing, URL state, export tools, transparent formulas, and responsive storytelling.

    Reproducibility, formulas, and limitations

    Data source

    DSC 106 GroceryDB CSV downloaded from the course project resources. The page analyzes all 26,250 product rows available in the file and keeps the original static visualization as a downloadable SVG.

    Download the local CSV | Download original SVG

    Cleaning logic

    Numeric fields are parsed from strings, HTML entities in product names are decoded, price per calorie is converted to price per 100 calories, sodium is displayed as mg per 100g, and price outliers are flagged with the 1.5 x IQR rule.

    Health Index

    Score = 100 - 26(FPro) - 8(NOVA/3) - sugar penalty - sodium penalty - calorie penalty + fiber bonus + protein bonus. The result is clamped to 0-100. This is an exploratory comparison score, not medical advice.

    Limitations

    NOVA/FPro values are model-derived, product listings are not purchases, prices may vary by region and time, categories are uneven, and product weights do not perfectly standardize serving behavior.
